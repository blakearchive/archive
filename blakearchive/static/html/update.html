31 August 2026

The William Blake Archive is pleased to announce the publication of Blake's annotations to Emanual Swedenborg's A Treatise Concerning Heaven and Hell and the Wonderful Things Therein. Bentley suggests that the marginalia was likely written between 1787 and 1790 (Writings, 2: 1742), placing its composition date near that of The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (c. 1790), if not somewhat simultaneous. The Marriage is, in part, a satirical response to Heaven and Hell and is perhaps one of Blake's best-known critiques on the socio-religious ideals of late 18th-century Britain. While Blake was briefly drawn to Swedenborgianism, it's in the margins of Heaven and Hell that we catch a glimpse into Blake's developing thoughts that emerge to oppose Swedenborg in the poet's subsequent work.

Swedenborg himself was an intriguing, if not enigmatic, figure. Born in Stockholm in 1688 to Jesper Swedberg, who eventually became a professor of theology himself, Emmanual showed a deep and innovative interest in topics like science, engineering, and cosmology during his early education and young adulthood. While science and spirituality were fundamentally entwined during the early enlightenment period, Swedenborg—already compelled by contrarian spiritual ideas—naturally transitioned from studying the natural world to focusing on his spiritual giftings. By the mid-1740s, he claimed to receive prophetic visions, dreams, and even psychic revelation regularly. One initiating vision in 1745, during which he described encountering Jesus, spurred him to eventually compose revelatory literature, including Heaven and Hell, published in 1758 (Swedenborg Foundation).

Swedenborg's prophetic phenomena are not dissimilar to Blake's own spiritual experiences, certainly sparking interest in the latter for the former's writings. Parallel to Blake's ideas on spirituality, Swedenborgianism also centered on a more egalitarian theology that attempted to revolutionize traditional Christianity, which generally focused on church attendance and regulating external behavior rather than, in Swedenborg's view, a spirit of equity and love, amongst other social and spiritual convictions. While Blake's annotations turned critical in Swedenborg's Divine Providence, he has annotated three books authored by the revelator, and possibly read two more. He and his wife also attended the first General Conference of the New Jerusalem in 1789, held in London.

Though initially interested in Swedenborg's revelations, Blake naturally resists the theosopher's inclination to systematize his visions of spiritual realms into dogma. Blake finds spiritual authenticity in fluidity between dualities, rather than rigid descriptions. In the right margin of Object 4, we see Blake contemplatively weighing out Swedenborg's words on the multiplicity of hell and its relationship to heaven; Blake writes, ""under every Good / is a hell"" and ""hell is the outward / or external / of heaven"". This harmony of opposites is brought to fruition in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell: ""Without Contraries is no progression. Attraction / Repulsion, Reason and Energy, Love and / Hate, are necessary to Human existence"" (Plate 3, Copy G) whereby Blake clearly departs from Swedenborg's firm separation of good and evil.

While only a few objects, this marginalia installment promises Archive readers engaging morsels sparked by an author who deeply impacted Blake and his work.
