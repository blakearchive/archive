The William Blake Archive is pleased to announce the publication of Blake’s annotations to Emanual Swedenborg's A Treatise Concerning Heaven and Hell and the Wonderful Things Therein. Bentley suggests that the marginalia was likely written between 1787 and 1790 (Bentley, Writings, 2: 1742), placing its composition date near that of The Marriage of Heaven and Hell of 1790. The Marriage is, in part, a satirical response to Swedenborg's Heaven and Hell and is one of Blake's best known and most accessible critiques of the religious ideals of late 18th-century Britain. It is here, in the margins of Swedenborg's text, that we catch a glimpse into Blake's emerging thoughts that later manifest in The Marriage and other works. Swedenborg himself figures into Blake's Marriage on Plate 3 as "the Angel sitting at the tomb" of Christ, "his writings are the linen clothes folded up." He returns on Plates 21 – 24, where Blake criticizes him directly as a plagiarist: "Thus Swedenborg boasts that what he writes is new; tho' it is only the Contents or Index of already publish'd books" (Plate 21).

Swedenborg (1688 – 1772) was an innovative scientist, engineer, and contrarian philosopher who began having visions in the last twenty years of his life. He published Heaven and Hell in Latin in 1758 and more than a dozen other theological texts. In January 1784, the printer Robert Hindmarsh formed "The Theosophical Society" with other followers of Swedenborg for translating and publishing the master's works in English, three of which Blake annotated. His annotations to the English translation of Heaven and Hell appear in its second edition of 1784.

Swedenborgianism centered on an egalitarian theology that attempted to revolutionize traditional Christianity, which generally focused on church attendance and regulating external behavior rather than, in Swedenborg's view, a spirit of equity and love. While initially interested in Swedenborg's revelations, Blake resists the theosopher's inclination to systematize his visions of spiritual realms into dogma. Blake finds spiritual authenticity in fluidity between dualities, rather than rigid descriptions. In the right margin of Object 4, Blake writes, "under every Good is a hell" and "hell is the outward or external of heaven". This theme is poetically expanded on in The Marriage of Heaven and Hell: "Without Contraries is no progression. Attraction and Repulsion, Reason and Energy, Love and Hate, are necessary to Human existence" (Plate 3). While brief, Blake's marginal responses to Swedenborg's Heaven and Hell offer glimpses into some of Blake's most contrarian and antinomian concepts.
